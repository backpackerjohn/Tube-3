Ten fifty p.m., July 5th, 2013. A freight train stops for the night on the main track at Nantes, Québec — five locomotives and seventy-two tank cars of crude oil, ten thousand two hundred and eighty-seven tons, parked on a descending grade. [S2][S16] One man runs this train. [S2] He sets seven hand brakes, then does what the rulebook requires: a test to prove the hand brakes alone will hold. [S2] The test passes. It is also wrong — because the locomotive's air brakes are still on, and the test can't tell him which brakes are doing the holding. [S2][S3]

In about two hours, the air will be gone. [S2]

## ACT 1 — THE SYSTEM AS DESIGNED (0:42–3:42 · 451 words)

[VISUAL — TITLE CARD: `MODE OF FAILURE` wordmark, Archivo Black, over the D1 linework; 6 px red underline on FAILURE. Cut to CONTEXT LOWER-THIRD: `LAC-MÉGANTIC, QUÉBEC · MMA RAILWAY · JULY 2013`.]

Lac-Mégantic is a lakeside town of about six thousand people in Québec's Eastern Townships, and a railway runs through the middle of it — a single track that curves right past the downtown core. [S2][S18] In 2013 that track belongs to the Montreal, Maine and Atlantic Railway: a short-line carrier stitched together from a bankrupt predecessor, running lean on old locomotives and deferred spending. [S3][S9]

And in 2013, this quiet line is suddenly carrying something new. The fracking boom in North Dakota's Bakken field is producing more crude oil than the pipelines can move, so the oil gets trains. [S16] Train MMA-002 is one of them: loaded at New Town, North Dakota, bound for the Irving Oil refinery in Saint John, New Brunswick — seven point seven million litres of crude in seventy-two tank cars. [S2][S16]

[VISUAL — DIAGRAM D2 "The Consist and the Grade": full train profile in cyan — 5 locomotives, 1 special-purpose caboose, 1 buffer boxcar, 72 tank cars; Plex Mono dimensions `4,701 FT · 10,287 TONS`. Below it, the elevation profile Nantes → Lac-Mégantic, green descent vector, label `AVG GRADE ≈ 1.2% · ~7 MI (11.6 KM)`. Citation: `Source: TSB R13D0054; NTSB Safety Compass (redrawn)`.]

Look at the machine itself, because three of its design facts decide everything. First: the tank cars are Class 111s — the general-purpose workhorse of North American rails, a design never meant for large volumes of flammable cargo, with a shell that punctures in derailments. Regulators on both sides of the border had been flagging exactly that weakness for years. [S16][S3]

Second: the cargo's paperwork. Crude oil ships as a Class 3 flammable liquid, sorted into packing groups by how dangerous it is. This oil is declared Packing Group III — the least hazardous category. [S8] Keep that in mind.

Third: the brakes. A freight train has three separate braking systems, and this whole story lives in the differences between them. [S1][S2] The automatic brakes run the length of the train on compressed air. The independent brakes are air brakes too, but they act on the locomotives only. And the hand brakes are the only system that needs no air at all — a wheel you crank, car by car, that clamps the brake shoes on mechanically and holds as long as steel holds. [S1][S2]

Air leaks. That is why the securement rule — Canada's Rule 112 — is built on the hand brakes: a train left unattended must be held by hand brakes *alone*, and the crew must prove it with a test — release the air, and see if the hand brakes hold the train. [S2][S3][S6]

[VISUAL — D2 stage 2: at the Nantes end of the profile, a parked-train icon; Plex Mono annotation box, Paper White: `MMA PRACTICE: PARK ON MAIN TRACK · UNATTENDED · ENGINE RUNNING · ONE-MAN CREW`. Stated in mono type, not dramatized.]

Here is how MMA operates this line. Trains routinely tie up for the night at Nantes — on the main track, on the grade, unattended, with one locomotive left running all night to keep the air-brake system charged. [S2][S10] All of it permitted. And since 2012, with Transport Canada's knowledge, MMA has run these oil trains with a crew of exactly one person. [S2][S10] One person to drive, one person to secure ten thousand tons — and one person to be the only check on his own arithmetic.

## ACT 2 — THE MECHANISM (3:42–9:42 · 901 words)

[VISUAL — timeline strip along bottom in Plex Mono: `JULY 5 2013 · 22:50`. Above, D2's parked consist at Nantes. CONTEXT LOWER-THIRD: `NANTES, QUÉBEC · 22:50 · 7 MILES FROM LAC-MÉGANTIC`.]

July 5th. The engineer brings MMA-002 up from Farnham alone, and the lead locomotive — MMA 5017 — is running badly: surging, and pushing black and white smoke from its stack. [S2][S4] At ten fifty p.m. he stops at Nantes, where his shift ends. [S2]

He shuts down the four trailing locomotives and leaves 5017 running, smoke and all, to feed the air brakes. [S2] He applies hand brakes on seven vehicles: the five locomotives, the special-purpose caboose, and the buffer boxcar. Not one tank car. [S1][S2] MMA's own instructions call for a minimum of nine hand brakes on a train this size. [S1][S2]

[VISUAL — DIAGRAM D3 "The Test That Lied," sequence per §3.3 rule 4, three stacked frames. `STATE 1 — THE TEST AS DONE`: consist held by hand-brake icons AND independent-brake air icons together, both engaged; a force bar in cyan reads HOLDING. `STATE 2 — WHAT IT SEEMED TO PROVE`: hand brakes alone, force bar still full — drawn ghosted, labeled `ASSUMED`. `STATE 3 — THE ACTUAL ARITHMETIC`: air icons removed, hand-brake force bar collapses to a red sliver; Plex Mono: `SET: 7 · MMA MINIMUM: 9 · REQUIRED (TSB TESTS): 17–26`. Citation: `Source: TSB R13D0054 (redrawn)`.]

Then comes the test. The rule says: prove the hand brakes hold the train by themselves. But the engineer leaves the independent brakes applied while he runs it. [S2][S3] The train doesn't move — of course it doesn't. It is being held by the hand brakes *plus* the locomotives' air brakes, and the test has no way of saying which. [S2][S3] The TSB later put real numbers on that hidden gap: without the air, holding this train on this grade needed a minimum of seventeen hand brakes, and possibly as many as twenty-six — depending on how hard each one was cranked. [S2][S3] Seven were set, and when investigators examined the wheels afterward, the heat marks on the treads indicated several of those seven had not been applied with full force. [S1][S19]

One more defect is hiding in the consist, and it's worth thirty seconds, because almost nobody tells this part. On these locomotives, applying the hand brake is supposed to trip a small device — a quick-release valve, the QRB — that dumps the air out of that axle's brake cylinder so the mechanical brake can seat properly. On the second locomotive, MMA 5026, that valve was defective, plugged by a part that didn't belong in it. Its hand brake, one of the seven, was delivering almost no force at all. [S5]

The engineer calls the rail traffic controller, reports the smoking engine, and they agree: leave the train as it is, deal with the locomotive in the morning. [S2] He takes a taxi to a hotel in Lac-Mégantic. [S2][S10] The train sits on the hill in the dark, one sick engine idling, held by air that is only there while that engine keeps turning.

[VISUAL — DIAGRAM D4 "One Engine, One Fire": cutaway of the 5017 diesel in cyan; the R4/R5 cam bearing marked; an amber oil-path arrow climbs from the failed bearing through the cylinder and intake to the turbocharger. The single Signal Red mark: the turbocharger ignition point. Inset: `NON-STANDARD REPAIR — POLYMERIC MATERIAL, OCT 2012`. Citation: `Source: TSB LP181/2013 (redrawn)`.]

At eleven forty, the reason for the smoke declares itself: fire, at the top of 5017's stack. [S2] Months earlier, the engine had broken down and been repaired on the cheap — a cam bearing seated in a polymeric filler, an epoxy-type material with nowhere near the strength the job required. [S3][S4] It failed in service. Engine oil found its way up through the damaged power assembly into the body of the turbocharger, pooled there, and got hot enough to ignite. [S4]

At about eleven fifty, a local resident dials 911. [S2] The Nantes fire department does its job exactly by the book: to starve a fuel-fed engine fire, they shut off the fuel and cut the locomotive down — following MMA's own emergency instructions. [S2][S3] The fire goes out. A track foreman sent by MMA meets them; they confer with the rail traffic controller; and with the fire dead and the train apparently secure, everyone leaves, shortly after midnight. [S2][S10]

The firefighters did the right thing. But now no engine is running on MMA-002 — and no engine means no compressor. [S2][S3]

[VISUAL — D1 returns, full build — the money shot. The independent-brake air gauge drawn as a cyan bar, bleeding down in steps; Plex Mono counter `≈1 PSI / MIN`. Beside it, the stacked holding-force bar: air portion shrinking, the small hand-brake portion static, the red gap between force-available and force-required widening. Timestamp advances `23:57 → 00:56`. Citation: `Source: TSB R13D0054 (redrawn)`.]

What happens over the next hour is not dramatic. It is a leak. The brake system loses air at roughly one pound per square inch every minute, and as the pressure falls, the independent brakes' grip on the locomotive wheels quietly relaxes. [S1][S2] Seven hand brakes — really six and a fraction — wait underneath as the floor comes down to meet them. [S2][S5]

Just before one in the morning, the arithmetic crosses zero. The combined force of the fading air brakes and the seven hand brakes drops below the pull of gravity on ten thousand tons, and MMA-002 — no crew, no lights, no one aware — starts to roll. [S2][S3]

It has seven miles of descending grade to Lac-Mégantic, and it covers them in about a quarter of an hour, accelerating the whole way. [S2] There is nothing on this stretch of railway designed to stop a train that no one is driving — no derail, no runaway track. [S3][S7] By the time the tank cars reach the curve at the western edge of downtown — a curve of four and a quarter degrees, the sharpest on this piece of track — the train is moving at sixty-five miles per hour. [S1][S2]

At about one fifteen a.m., sixty-three of the seventy-two tank cars derail in the middle of town. [S2]

[VISUAL — ARCHIVAL A1: distant, non-graphic wide shot of the fire over the town skyline at night (Wikimedia Commons, license verified per manifest — gate 3), duotone-framed, held briefly and soberly. CITATION LOWER-THIRD: `LAC-MÉGANTIC · 06 JULY 2013 · SOURCE: SEE MANIFEST`. No zooms, no music stab.]

Class 111 shells breach. About six million litres of crude oil release almost at once, and it does not behave like the paperwork said it would. Packing Group III oil is not supposed to explode; this oil ignites and detonates in fireballs, because — as TSB testing later showed — its flash point was below minus thirty-five degrees Celsius and its volatility was comparable to gasoline. [S2][S8] The fires destroy forty buildings and fifty-three vehicles; some two thousand people are evacuated. [S1][S2][S18]

Forty-seven people die that night. [S2][S3] It is Canada's deadliest rail disaster since 1864. [S11] They were in their homes and their town on an ordinary warm Friday night, and this video will spend the rest of its time on the only thing an investigation can offer them: the exact, complete anatomy of why. [S3]

## MID-VIDEO RE-HOOK (9:42–10:09 · 67 words)

[VISUAL — hard cut to text card on navy, Archivo Semibold, Paper White: `SEVEN HAND BRAKES EXPLAIN THE RUNAWAY.` beat. Second line: `THEY DON'T EXPLAIN THE RAILWAY.` Grid only.]

Seven hand brakes explain the runaway. They do not explain the railway. When the TSB finished — thirteen months, one of the largest investigations in its history — it did not publish a cause. It published *eighteen* of them: eighteen distinct causes and contributing factors, plus sixteen more findings about risk, stacked so that removing almost any one of them breaks the chain. [S3][S19] Here is that chain.

## ACT 3 — THE DECISION CHAIN & THE SYSTEM (10:09–13:35 · 515 words)

[VISUAL — DIAGRAM D5 "Eighteen Factors": a causal map in house style — plain boxes, 90° leader lines, filling in as narrated, grouped in three Plex Mono bands: `THE MACHINE`, `THE COMPANY`, `THE REGULATOR`. The single red element: the final node, `RUNAWAY`. Citation: `Source: TSB R13D0054, findings (structure ours)`.]

Start with the machine. The engine fire traces to a repair: a failed cam bearing rebuilt with a polymeric material that lacked the strength and durability the application required — a non-standard fix that put a fire-prone locomotive at the head of an oil train. [S3][S4] The defective quick-release valve on 5026 subtracted one hand brake from seven. [S5] And the tank cars: the Class 111's known vulnerability didn't cause the runaway, but the TSB found it made the consequences catastrophically worse once the cars left the rails. [S3][S16]

Then the company. The securement failure at Nantes was not one man's bad night; the TSB found MMA was a company with a weak safety culture, one that did not have a functioning safety-management system to identify and manage the risks in its own operation. [S3][S9] Employees were not adequately trained, tested, or supervised on securement; leaving trains on the main track at Nantes, on the grade, unattended, was routine practice. [S3][S2] Single-person operation had been introduced the year before — and here the report is careful in a way worth quoting the spirit of: the TSB examined whether a second crew member would have prevented the accident, and could not conclude that it would have. [S1][S10] What it did conclude is that MMA introduced the change without adequately assessing and managing its risks. [S3]

And the shipper: the crude that behaved like gasoline had been classified Packing Group III — the mildest category — so its true volatility was never priced into how it was handled. [S2][S8]

Then the regulator. Transport Canada knew this railway. Its inspectors had found securement problems and other recurring defects at MMA for years — but the TSB found the department did not audit MMA often enough or deeply enough to confirm the company was actually managing the risks, and when problems repeated, follow-up didn't come. [S3][S9] The safety-management system that should have caught all of this existed on paper at MMA, and the oversight that should have caught the empty paper was thin. [S3]

The chair of the TSB, Wendy Tadros, put the finding in one sentence when the report was released: "Accidents never come down to a single individual, a single action or a single factor. You have to look at the whole context." [S3]

[VISUAL — ARCHIVAL A2: the R13D0054 report cover shown as a document beside our redrawn D5 — the "redrawn" claim shown literally. CITATION LOWER-THIRD: `TSB R13D0054 · RELEASED 19 AUG 2014 · 18 CAUSES & CONTRIBUTING FACTORS`.]

What changed is the payoff of this story. Seventeen days after the derailment, Transport Canada issued an emergency directive: no more one-person crews on trains carrying dangerous goods, no more leaving such trains unattended on main tracks, locked cabs, removed reversers, and hard rules on brakes for anything left standing. [S6][S10] Rule 112 — the securement rule the test at Nantes hollowed out — was rewritten: a defined hand-brake chart instead of judgment, and securement confirmed with a second, knowledgeable employee. [S6] The oldest Class 111s were ordered out of dangerous-goods service within a year, and by 2015 Canada and the United States had a tougher joint tank-car standard, TC-117, with a retrofit schedule running past 2025. [S16][S17] And on the TSB's recommendation, railways now must layer *physical* defences against runaways — and Transport Canada must audit safety-management systems deeply enough to know whether they actually work. [S7][S3]

## CLOSE (13:35–14:55 · 195 words)

[VISUAL — return to hero diagram D1, fully labeled, held a beat; the labels fade until only the red gap in the holding-force bar remains on the navy field.]

The rest of the ledger, briefly and plainly. MMA was bankrupt within a month. [S13] Three of its employees — the engineer, the rail traffic controller, and the operations manager — stood trial for criminal negligence; in January 2018, a jury acquitted all three. [S12] The town is still rebuilding, and the bypass meant to take freight trains out of downtown Lac-Mégantic was, as of the disaster's thirteenth anniversary this month, still not finished. [S14] Uncontrolled train movements remain on the TSB's Watchlist of Canada's most persistent safety issues — the problem is smaller, and it is not gone. [S15]

A train is held on a hill by simple physics: force applied against force available. Every layer that was supposed to check that arithmetic — the test, the rules, the company, the regulator — checked something else instead. That is what eighteen causes look like. [S3]
