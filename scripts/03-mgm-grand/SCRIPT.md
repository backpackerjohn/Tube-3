> **Fact-check note (pre-resolved):** The slate's working title "The Fire Was Out in Minutes…" did **not** survive sourcing. No official source states the fire was "out in minutes" — the casino flashed over within ~6 minutes of discovery and crews fought it through the morning [S2][S3]. Per prompt 7 / playbook K3 (same class of catch as Ep 01's "thirteen seconds"), the claim is gone from title and script. The defensible paradox is confinement, not duration: the flames stayed essentially on the casino level while the smoke killed on floors 16–26 [S2][S6][S15].

## ACT 1 — THE SYSTEM AS DESIGNED (0:44–3:27 · 407 words)

[VISUAL — TITLE CARD: `MODE OF FAILURE` wordmark, Archivo Black, over the D1 linework; 6 px red underline on FAILURE. Cut to CONTEXT LOWER-THIRD: `LAS VEGAS, NEVADA / THE STRIP · OPENED DECEMBER 5, 1973`.]

December 5th, 1973. The MGM Grand opens on the Las Vegas Strip: a hundred and six million dollars, twenty-six stories, two thousand and eighty-four rooms — at its opening, the largest hotel in the world. [S5][S14] The ground floor is a small city: the main casino, restaurants, showrooms, a jai alai arena, a movie theater. [S5] Above it all, the tower of guest rooms.

[VISUAL — ARCHIVAL A2: pre-fire period view of the 1973 MGM Grand exterior — postcard or promotional photo, **use only if PD-verified** (pre-1978 US publication without copyright notice, checked on the Wikimedia Commons file page — gate 3); fallback is a self-drawn stylized elevation in house linework. Duotone toward Report Navy; the photo unaltered in what it depicts. CONTEXT LOWER-THIRD: `MGM GRAND HOTEL · LARGEST HOTEL IN THE WORLD, 1973`.]

The building has sprinklers — in places. [S2][S4] The main casino is not one of them. Neither is The Deli, the restaurant where this story starts. [S2][S4] That is not an oversight, and it was not hidden. The code Clark County applied during design allowed an exemption: spaces occupied around the clock could go unsprinklered, on the theory that a room full of people is its own fire detector — someone will always spot a fire while it's small, raise the alarm, reach for an extinguisher. [S2][S4] The casino qualified; it never closes. The Deli was granted a variance on the same logic, because the plans said it would serve customers twenty-four hours a day. [S4] Hold on to that sentence. It has an expiration date nobody wrote down.

[VISUAL — DIAGRAM D2 "The Dry Floor": plan view of the casino level in cyan linework; sprinklered zones hatched in Gauge-Green-free neutral cyan fill — this is the amber class, so protected zones get a Paper White hatch, unprotected zones stay open linework. Plex Mono labels: `SPRINKLERED` / `NOT SPRINKLERED — 24-HR OCCUPANCY EXEMPTION`. The Deli's footprint sits inside the unprotected zone; no red yet. Citation: `Source: NFPA MGM Grand investigation report (redrawn)`.]

During construction, full sprinkler coverage was priced: about a hundred and ninety-two thousand dollars, on a hundred-and-six-million-dollar project — under a fifth of one percent. [S11] A consultant's letter reportedly warned the owners about, quote, "the liability of all the unsprinklered areas in this building." [S11] The full coverage was not built. Under the code, it did not have to be. [S2][S4]

The interiors are 1973 luxury, which means 1973 chemistry: plastic laminates, foam-padded furnishings, PVC piping, decorative finishes — and above the casino's ornate ceiling, a concealed space packed with wiring, ductwork, and more plastic. [S2][S3][S4]

[VISUAL — D1 returns, stage 3: the tower cutaway annotates its three plants, one at a time, in Plex Mono callout tags 1-2-3: (1) two thin vertical gaps drawn through the full tower height, label `SEISMIC JOINTS — TO BE SEALED`; (2) a magnified guest-room inset: door, grille above it, fan-coil unit, label `MAKEUP AIR: CORRIDOR → ROOM`; (3) a small panel icon at the base, label `FIRE ALARM: MANUAL`. All cyan; the red deli dot persists. Citation: `Source: NFPA / CCFD reports (redrawn)`.]

Now the tower — because three of its design details are where the deaths will happen. First: the high-rise is built in sections separated by seismic joints, vertical gaps running the full height of the building so the structure can flex in an earthquake. On paper, they are sealed against fire and smoke. [S2][S4] Second: every guest room breathes from the corridor — its heating-and-cooling unit pulls makeup air through a grille above the room door. The corridor's air is the room's air. [S2][S4] Third: the alarm. There is no automatic fire-detection system waiting over the casino, and no alarm will sound in this tower without human hands. [S2][S13]

For seven years, none of this matters. The hotel fills, empties, and fills again, roughly five thousand people at a time. [S5][S14]

## ACT 2 — THE MECHANISM (3:27–9:13 · 865 words)

[VISUAL — DIAGRAM D3 "The Wall That Was Wiring," reveal stage 1: cutaway of The Deli's serving-station wall in cyan linework. Inside: a pie display case, its compressor, an evaporator fan, and a run of flexible conduit snaking through the concealed stud space. Plex Mono labels: `PIE CASE` · `COMPRESSOR` · `FLEXIBLE METAL CONDUIT (ALUMINUM)` · `REFRIGERANT LINE (COPPER)`. Citation: `Source: CCFD fire investigation report (redrawn)`.]

Inside The Deli's serving station, behind a wall, is a piece of wiring nobody has thought about in years. [S1] It feeds the refrigeration unit of a pie display case — a compressor and an evaporator fan — spliced as an extension onto the station's original circuits. [S1][S4] The investigation will find that extension was never properly grounded: no grounding conductor inside the flexible metal conduit. The installers left the conduit itself to do that job. [S1][S2][S4]

[VISUAL — D3 stage 2: magnified detail where the aluminum conduit crosses the copper refrigerant line — contact point circled (still cyan). Micro-vibration marks drawn as short kit-style motion ticks at the compressor. Plex Mono: `ALUMINUM × COPPER · COMPRESSOR VIBRATION · GALVANIC ACTION`. Reveal 3: the contact point turns Signal Red — the episode's single red element now lives here. Label: `GROUND PATH LOST → FAULT ARCS IN CONCEALED SPACE`.]

And the conduit has a problem of its own. It is aluminum, and it lies against the copper line carrying refrigerant to the compressor. Two dissimilar metals in contact, vibrated day and night, corroding where they touch — galvanic action. [S1][S4] Somewhere in that wall, the conduit's electrical continuity is eaten through. So when aging, heat-baked insulation finally lets a conductor touch metal, the fault current has no clean path home. It arcs. It heats. [S1][S4] And the space it heats is combustible, concealed, unsprinklered, and watched by no detector of any kind. [S1][S2]

How long the fire grows in there is one of the investigation's honest blanks. The Deli is closed at that hour — remember the twenty-four-hour premise — the space is sealed, and no one can see in. [S1][S4]

[VISUAL — timeline strip along the bottom in Plex Mono begins: `NOV 21 1980 · 7:05`. Above it, D2 plan view returns; a small red glow marks The Deli. CONTEXT LOWER-THIRD: `FRIDAY, NOVEMBER 21, 1980 · 7:05 A.M.`]

At about 7:05 in the morning, employees near The Deli notice a flicker where no light should be. One of them looks into the restaurant and finds flame moving along the wall and ceiling. [S3][S14] Security is called; workers go at it with extinguishers — but it is already above them, in the ceiling space, running. [S1][S3] The first telephone call reaches the Clark County Fire Department at 7:17. The first engine arrives at 7:19. Two minutes. [S3]

It does not matter.

[VISUAL — D2 plan view, the money sequence, per §3.3 rule 4: three stacked frames, `7:05 — THE DELI` / `~7:20 — FLASHOVER SPREADS` / `7:25 — CASINO + PORTE-COCHÈRE INVOLVED`. Fire's reach rendered as Caution Amber `#E0A458` fill sweeping the plan — flow fronts and arrows per Template B, never illustrated flames. A Plex Mono speed annotation runs with the front: `SPREAD RATE: 15–19 FT/SEC`. Citation: `Source: CCFD / NFPA reports (redrawn)`.]

Because overhead, the fire has found the casino: a concealed ceiling full of burning plastics over an open floor of fuel. [S1][S2] Investigators later put the spread rate across the casino at fifteen to nineteen feet per second — across a gambling floor, faster than a sprint. [S2][S3] Within about six minutes of discovery, the main casino flashes over. [S3] Fire crews barely forty feet inside the entrance meet a fireball rolling over their heads; it blows out through the front doors and engulfs the porte-cochère — the grand covered driveway — by 7:25. [S3]

And then the geometry of this disaster inverts. In the casino, the flames have already done nearly all the killing they will do as flames. Crews will fight the fire through the morning, and it will never climb the tower: the burning stays, essentially, on the level where it was born. [S2][S15] If the story ended here, this would be a serious but contained hotel fire. What makes it the MGM Grand fire is what the burning produced — and where the building let it go. [S2]

[VISUAL — HERO DIAGRAM D1, full build — the signature frame. From the casino level, Caution Amber flow arrows begin to climb the tower cutaway by four routes, revealed one at a time as narrated, numbered kit tags: `1 SEISMIC JOINTS (UNSEALED)` · `2 ELEVATOR HOISTWAYS` · `3 STAIRWELLS` · `4 HVAC — DAMPERS FAILED`. The arrows thicken as they rise; floors 16–26 shade in a 30% amber wash. The deli dot stays the lone red mark. Citation: `Source: NFPA MGM Grand investigation report (redrawn)`.]

A fire this size, fed on synthetics, produces smoke loaded with carbon monoxide and the combustion products of burning plastics — by the building-ful. [S2][S5] Hot smoke rises. All it needs is a path. The building offers four. [S2][S4]

The seismic joints — those full-height gaps between the tower's sections — turn out not to be sealed. They run like flues from the casino ceiling toward the roof, and investigators found the heaviest death toll clustered near the building's core, where smoke moving up those joints was worst. [S2][S4][S6]

The elevator hoistways: more vertical shafts, opening onto every floor. [S2][S6]

The stairwells: opened, and held open, by the people trying to use them. [S2][S6]

And the ventilation system itself. The smoke dampers that should have closed the ductwork were installed in ways that meant they could not do their job, and parts of the system kept moving air — now smoke — through the tower. [S2][S4][S9]

[VISUAL — D4 "The Room That Breathes the Corridor": guest-room cutaway. Corridor at left filling with a 30% amber wash; the transfer grille above the door pulls a thin amber arrow into the room; a neutral Blueprint Blue scale silhouette stands at the window (no face, per §3.3 rule 5). Plex Mono: `MAKEUP AIR GRILLE — CORRIDOR SMOKE ENTERS CLOSED ROOMS`. Citation: `Source: NFPA report, HVAC findings (redrawn)`.]

Now remember how every guest room breathes: through the grille above its door, from the corridor. Once smoke owns a corridor, every grille on it feeds smoke into the closed rooms behind. [S2][S4] A locked door is not a seal; it is a wall with a vent over it.

No general alarm sounds in the tower — not then, not at any point that morning. [S13] Guests wake to the smell, to shouting in the hallways, to sirens twenty floors down.

What people did next is preserved in a federal behavioral study built from hundreds of survivor accounts. [S17] They soaked towels and packed them against doorsills. They broke windows for air. They climbed toward the roof — and found stairwell doors that locked behind them, and in some shafts, smoke instead of sky. [S2][S6][S17] Through the morning, helicopters — Air Force crews from Nellis, police, even news pilots — lifted survivors off the roof by the hundreds. [S3][S5]

[VISUAL — D1 held static. The amber arrows fade; Plex Mono figures set themselves beside the tower, one line at a time, ledger-style, on the navy field. This beat is numbers on a drawing — no photographs, no faces, per never-rule 1.]

Eighty-five people died. About seven hundred more were injured. [S2][S5] The coroner's findings state the mechanism with precision: seventy-nine of the eighty-five deaths were from smoke and carbon monoxide. Four involved burns at all. One was a failing heart; one was a fall during escape. [S5][S6] The map of where the dead were found says the rest: eighteen on the casino level — and sixty-one in the tower, on floors sixteen through twenty-six: twenty-five in guest rooms, twenty-two in corridors, nine in stairways, five in elevators. [S2][S6]

The fire never came within a dozen floors of most of them. The smoke did. [S2][S6]

## MID-VIDEO RE-HOOK (9:13–9:47 · 85 words)

[VISUAL — hard cut to text card on navy, Archivo Semibold, Paper White: `SOME OF WHAT FAILED WAS ILLEGAL.` beat. Second line: `THE BIGGEST GAP WAS LEGAL.` Grid only, no diagram.]

Here is the part that turned this fire into law. Some of what failed that morning was improper by any standard — an ungrounded circuit, joints left unsealed, dampers that couldn't close. [S1][S2][S4] But the biggest gap of all was perfectly legal: the casino had no sprinklers because the code said it didn't need them. [S2][S4] The MGM Grand was an approved, permitted, inspected building. To understand why eighty-five people died in it, you have to read the code — and the year stamped on its cover. [S2][S8]

## ACT 3 — THE DECISION CHAIN & THE SYSTEM (9:47–13:03 · 491 words)

[VISUAL — DIAGRAM D5 "The Decision Chain": vertical causal map in house style — six plain boxes joined by 90° leader lines, filled as narrated, Plex Mono text. The single red element: the final box. Citation: `Source: CCFD / NFPA findings (structure ours)`.]

Walk the chain the investigations laid out. [S1][S2]

Decision one, at design: build under a code that lets continuously occupied spaces go unsprinklered. Legal, and reasoned — people as the detection system. The reasoning dies the moment a fire starts inside a wall of a room that happens to be empty. [S2][S4] The Deli is that room. Its variance assumed a twenty-four-hour restaurant; by 1980, The Deli closed at night. The premise of the exemption had expired years earlier. The exemption had not. [S4] Nobody's job was to notice.

Decision two, priced and declined: one hundred ninety-two thousand dollars for full sprinkler coverage — a fifth of one percent of the project — set against a warning letter about exactly these unsprinklered areas. [S11] Fire-protection engineers have argued ever since that one working sprinkler head over that wall would likely have ended this story before the tower knew there was a fire. [S9][S10]

Then the construction chain, each link small. An equipment ground that wasn't there. [S1] Seismic joints specified sealed, left open. [S2][S4] Smoke dampers installed so they could not close. [S2][S9] A tower alarm that waited for human hands that never came. [S2][S13] Any one of these is a detail. The smoke used all of them, in order.

And over everything, the systemic condition: time. The MGM Grand answered to the code in force when it was approved — and to no code passed since. Rules only reached forward, to buildings not yet built. Every year of fire-safety progress after 1973 stopped at this building's front door. [S8][S10]

[VISUAL — ARCHIVAL A3: the CCFD report cover and the NFPA report cover shown as documents on the navy field, then the NFPA report's own smoke-spread figure beside our redrawn D1 — the "redrawn" claim shown literally. CITATION LOWER-THIRD: `CLARK COUNTY FIRE DEPT / NFPA INVESTIGATION REPORTS · 1981–82 · PUBLIC RECORD`.]

The correction was fast — and it took a second fire to lock it in. Within a week, Nevada's governor convened a commission on whether older hotels should be forced up to modern standards. [S8] While it deliberated, on February 10th, 1981 — eighty-one days after the MGM — the Las Vegas Hilton burned. Eight more people died, and once again the killer in a high-rise was smoke. [S7][S8] The argument was over. In 1981, Nevada enacted one of the strictest retrofit laws in the country: automatic sprinklers, smoke detectors in rooms and elevators, posted exit plans — required *backward*, into thousands of buildings that already existed. [S7][S8][S12]

[VISUAL — D1 final state: the tower cutaway re-draws itself with sprinkler-head ticks on every level and sealed-joint hatching; the amber is gone. Plex Mono: `RETROFIT — NEVADA, 1981`. Then a wider frame: rows of small building outlines across the state, each gaining a tick.]

The MGM Grand itself reopened in July 1981, after an eight-month, fifty-million-dollar reconstruction, carrying more than thirty thousand sprinkler heads. [S12][S15] Nationally, the fire rewrote the model codes through the 1980s: smoke-damper installation and testing, alarm requirements, concealed combustible spaces, high-rise smoke control — with the MGM cited, in the fire-protection literature, as the turning point. [S9][S10] And in 1990, Congress added a quiet lever: the Hotel and Motel Fire Safety Act, under which federal employees on travel may only be lodged in properties meeting its fire-safety requirements — sprinklers included. A guest list as leverage. [S16]

Nevada went from the state where a casino could argue its way out of sprinklers to a national reference point for hotel fire safety. [S7][S8] That inversion — law reaching backward into existing buildings — is this fire's signature contribution to the code. [S8][S10]

## CLOSE (13:03–14:03 · 149 words)

[VISUAL — return to hero diagram D1, fully labeled, held a beat. The labels fade until only the small red mark at The Deli remains on the navy field.]

The tower still stands on the Strip. It became Bally's in 1985; today it operates as the Horseshoe. [S14] Guests there sleep under sprinkler heads on every ceiling — including the casino's. [S12]

Two numbers to keep from this one. Fifteen to nineteen feet per second: how fast fire crossed a casino floor that had been left unsprinklered, legally. [S2][S3] And sixty-one: the people the smoke killed on floors the fire never touched — through joints, shafts, grilles, and dampers, every path built by a decision, none of them drawn by the fire. [S2][S6]
